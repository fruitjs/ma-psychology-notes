    Psychology of Personality · Unit I

    Personality: Meaning, Determinants and Body Types

    What is personality?

    The word comes from the Latin persona, the mask worn by actors in Roman theatre. It meant the role a person shows to the world. Psychologists today mean much more than outward appearance: personality is the whole pattern of how a person typically thinks, feels and behaves.

    Definitions to quote in the exam

    Gordon Allport (1937)

    "Personality is the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment."

    The most-quoted definition. Learn it word for word.

    Hans Eysenck

    "The more or less stable and enduring organization of a person's character, temperament, intellect and physique, which determines his unique adjustment to the environment."

    Feist & Feist

    "A pattern of relatively permanent traits and unique characteristics that give both consistency and individuality to a person's behaviour."

    Unpacking Allport's definition

    Characteristics of personality

    Unique

    No two people have exactly the same personality, not even identical twins.

    Consistent and stable

    It shows across time and situations, so others can predict us.

    Dynamic

    It still changes slowly with age and experience.

    Psychophysical

    It involves both mind (thoughts, emotions) and body (glands, nervous system, physique).

    Organized whole

    Traits work together as a system, not as separate pieces.

    Shaped by both nature and nurture

    It comes from heredity and environment acting together.

    RememberU-C-D-P-O-N: "Uncle Can't Drive Properly On NH-1." (Unique, Consistent, Dynamic, Psychophysical, Organized, Nature + nurture)

    Determinants of personality

    A determinant is anything that shapes how personality develops. They fall into two big families. The answer to "nature or nurture?" is always both, interacting.

    A. Biological determinants

    Heredity

    Genes passed from parents set our basic temperament, intelligence potential and physical make-up. Twin and adoption studies show identical twins are more alike in personality than fraternal twins, even when raised apart.

    Nervous system

    The brain and autonomic nervous system affect how easily we get aroused, excited or calm. For example, a highly reactive nervous system relates to anxiety.

    Physique

    Height, weight, looks and health affect how others treat us and how we see ourselves. Sheldon and Kretschmer built whole theories on this.

    Brain chemistry

    Neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin are linked to sensation-seeking, mood and impulsivity.

    Endocrine glands and personality

    GlandHormoneToo muchToo little
    ThyroidThyroxineRestless, anxious, irritable, can't sleepSluggish, tired, slow thinking; in children, cretinism
    AdrenalAdrenaline, cortisolConstant stress, tension, aggressionWeakness, low energy, poor stress coping
    Pituitary ("master gland")Growth hormone, controls other glandsGigantismDwarfism; affects self-image
    Gonads (testes / ovaries)Testosterone, oestrogenLinked to dominance and aggression (testosterone)Delayed sexual maturity, low drive
    ParathyroidParathormone (calcium balance)Lethargy, low excitabilityOver-excitability, muscle spasms

    B. Socio-cultural determinants

    Family

    The first and strongest influence. Parents are role models, and the emotional climate of the home shapes trust, confidence and values.

    Birth order and siblings

    Adler said firstborns tend to be responsible, middle children competitive, youngest children pampered. Research support is mixed.

    School, teachers, peers

    Teachers give feedback that shapes self-esteem; peers shape social skills, especially in adolescence.

    Culture

    Individualistic cultures (USA) value independence and self-expression. Collectivistic cultures (India, Japan) value family duty, harmony and interdependence.

    Religion and values

    Teach moral rules, self-control, and meaning in life.

    Social class, gender roles, media

    Affect opportunities, expectations, role models and the "ideal" person a child copies.

    Parenting styles (Baumrind; Maccoby & Martin)

    High warmth
    Low warmth
    High control
    AuthoritativeClear rules with love and reasons. Child becomes confident, responsible, socially skilled.
    AuthoritarianStrict, "because I said so". Child becomes obedient but anxious, low self-esteem.
    Low control
    PermissiveLoving but few rules. Child becomes impulsive, poor self-control.
    NeglectfulUninvolved. Child shows the poorest outcomes: insecurity, behaviour problems.

    Constitutional theory
    William H. Sheldon
    Lived
    1898–1977, American psychologist and physician
    Theory
    Somatotype theory of personality (body type → temperament)
    Key works
    The Varieties of Human Physique (1940); The Varieties of Temperament (1942); Atlas of Men (1954)
    Method
    Standardised photographs of about 4,000 male college students

    "Your body build tells us about your temperament."

    The three somatotypes and their temperaments

    Sheldon named the body components after the three layers of the embryo, because each layer develops into different body tissues.

    Body typePhysiqueTemperamentTypical traits
    EndomorphySoft, round, large stomach; well-developed digestive organsViscerotoniaLove of comfort and food, relaxed, sociable, even-tempered, needs affection
    MesomorphyMuscular, broad shoulders, strong bonesSomatotoniaAssertive, adventurous, energetic, likes risk and power, can be aggressive
    EctomorphyThin, long limbs, delicate; large brain and nervous system relative to bodyCerebrotoniaRestrained, self-conscious, sensitive, likes privacy, intellectual

    The 7-point rating: somatotype numbers

    Sheldon did not put people in one box. He rated every person on all three components from 1 (low) to 7 (high), always in the order endo – meso – ecto.

    7 – 1 – 1

    Extreme endomorph

    1 – 7 – 1

    Extreme mesomorph

    1 – 1 – 7

    Extreme ectomorph

    4 – 4 – 4

    Balanced, average build

    In his temperament study Sheldon followed 200 young men for about 5 years and rated them on 60 traits (20 per temperament). He reported correlations of about .80 between body type and temperament, which is very high.

    Evaluation

    Contributions

    • Used dimensions (ratings) rather than rigid types
    • Tried to measure physique objectively with photographs
    • Started research on the body–personality link; in Varieties of Delinquent Youth (1949) he found delinquents tended to be mesomorphic

    Criticisms

    • Sheldon rated both body and temperament himself: strong rater bias
    • Later studies found much weaker links (about .20–.30)
    • Only male, mostly white college students were studied
    • Ignores environment: people may learn roles that fit their body (stereotypes)
    • Ethical problems with the nude posture photographs
    Constitutional theory
    Ernst Kretschmer
    Lived
    1888–1964, German psychiatrist (Universities of Tübingen and Marburg)
    Theory
    Constitutional theory: physique linked to temperament and mental illness
    Key work
    Körperbau und Charakter (Physique and Character, 1921)
    Method
    Measured the bodies of about 260 psychiatric patients and compared body type with diagnosis
    Photo
    Martha Conrad, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

    "Body type is linked to temperament, and in extreme cases to type of mental illness."

    Kretschmer's four body types

    TypeBodyTemperamentLinked disorder (if extreme)
    PyknicShort, round body, thick neck, broad face, fat around the trunkCyclothymic: sociable, warm, cheerful, moods swing between happy and sadManic-depressive psychosis (bipolar disorder)
    Asthenic (leptosome)Tall, thin, long limbs, narrow chest, weak musclesSchizothymic: shy, reserved, sensitive, unsociable, lives in own inner worldSchizophrenia
    AthleticMuscular, broad shoulders, strong bonesEnergetic, active, aggressive; calm and slow to react (later called "viscous")Grouped closer to schizophrenia in his data; later linked to epilepsy
    DysplasticBody parts out of proportion; does not fit any typeNo single patternOften grouped with schizophrenia
    RememberPyknic = Plump & Party (cyclothymic, mood swings) · Asthenic = Alone (schizothymic) · Athletic = Active · Dysplastic = Disproportionate

    Key idea: Kretschmer saw a continuum: normal temperament → borderline → mental illness. A cyclothymic person is a "mild" version of manic-depressive; a schizothymic person is a "mild" version of schizophrenic.

    Evaluation

    Contributions

    • One of the first systematic attempts to link body and mind
    • Influenced Sheldon's somatotype theory
    • Pointed to a continuum between normal and abnormal personality

    Criticisms

    • Age was not controlled: manic-depressive patients were older, and people get rounder with age
    • Based on psychiatric patients, so hard to apply to normal people
    • Rigid types; most people are mixed
    • Ignores social and learning factors

    Sheldon vs Kretschmer

    PointKretschmerSheldon
    Year19211940–42
    Country / fieldGerman psychiatristAmerican psychologist and physician
    SamplePsychiatric patients (about 260)Normal college men (about 4,000)
    ApproachTypes (put a person in one box)Dimensions (rate all three, 1–7)
    Body typesPyknic, asthenic, athletic, dysplasticEndomorph, mesomorph, ectomorph
    MatchingPyknic ≈ endomorph · Asthenic ≈ ectomorph · Athletic ≈ mesomorphViscerotonia, somatotonia, cerebrotonia

    Common criticism for both: correlation is not causation. Society's stereotypes (the "jolly fat person", the "nerdy thin person") may teach people to act the part.

    Type vs trait: why it matters

    Type theories (Kretschmer) sort people into separate categories. Trait or dimension theories (Sheldon's ratings, and later Eysenck, Cattell and the Big Five in Unit IV) place everyone somewhere along continuous scales. Modern psychology prefers dimensions because most people fall in the middle.

    Psychology of Personality · Unit II

    Psychodynamic Approaches

    The big idea

    "Psychodynamic" means personality is driven by inner mental forces (drives, conflicts, wishes), many of them unconscious. Our early childhood shapes adult personality.

    Freud started it. Jung, Adler and Horney were first his followers, then broke away. Each kept the unconscious but changed what drives us.

    Freud

    Id, ego, superego; psychosexual stages; defence mechanisms.

    Jung

    Archetypes; introvert / extravert; individuation.

    Adler

    Inferiority feelings; social interest; style of life; birth order.

    Horney

    Basic anxiety; moving toward, against, away from people.

    Psychoanalysis
    Sigmund Freud
    Lived
    1856–1939, born in Freiberg, Moravia (now Czech Republic); worked in Vienna; fled the Nazis to London in 1938
    Training
    Medical doctor (neurologist); learned hypnosis from Charcot; worked with Josef Breuer
    Key works
    Studies on Hysteria (1895, with Breuer); The Interpretation of Dreams (1900); The Ego and the Id (1923)
    Photo
    Max Halberstadt, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

    "The mind is like an iceberg: most of it is hidden beneath the surface."

    Levels of mental life (topographic model)

    Provinces of the mind (structural model)

    ID · "I want it now"

    Present at birth. Fully unconscious. Works on the pleasure principle and primary process thinking (illogical, wish-fulfilling).

    EGO · "Let's be realistic"

    Develops from the id. Works on the reality principle and secondary process (logical). The "executive" that balances id, superego and reality.

    SUPEREGO · "You should not"

    Develops around age 5 from parents' rules. Two parts: conscience (guilt for wrong) and ego-ideal (pride for right). Works on the moralistic/idealistic principle.

    RememberId = devil on one shoulder, Superego = angel on the other, Ego = you in the middle deciding.

    Dynamics: drives and anxiety

    Drives (instincts)

    Eros / sex (life instinct; its energy is libido) and Thanatos / aggression (death instinct). Each drive has an impetus, source, aim and object.

    Three types of anxiety

    Neurotic: fear of the id's impulses. Moral: fear of the superego (guilt). Realistic: fear of real danger.

    Defence mechanisms

    The ego unconsciously distorts reality to protect itself from anxiety.

    MechanismWhat happensExample
    RepressionPushing painful thoughts into the unconscious (the basic one)Forgetting a traumatic accident
    Reaction formationShowing the opposite of the real feelingBeing extra-sweet to someone you dislike
    DisplacementRedirecting feelings to a safer targetAngry at boss, shouting at younger brother
    FixationGetting stuck at an earlier stageAdult who constantly needs to chew or smoke
    RegressionGoing back to earlier behaviour under stressOlder child starts bed-wetting when a sibling is born
    ProjectionSeeing your own unwanted feelings in others"She hates me" when you actually dislike her
    IntrojectionTaking in another person's qualitiesChild copies a feared parent's behaviour
    SublimationChannelling drives into socially valued activity (the healthy one)Aggression turned into competitive sport
    RememberR-R-D-F-R-P-I-S: "Ram Rani Did Find Real Peace In Shimla."

    Psychosexual stages

    Oral0–1 yr

    Pleasure from mouth: sucking, biting. Fixation → dependence, smoking, overeating, sarcasm ("biting" remarks).

    Anal1–3 yrs

    Pleasure from holding/expelling; toilet training. Fixation → anal character: orderly, stingy, stubborn (or the opposite: messy).

    Phallic3–5 yrs

    Focus on genitals. Oedipus complex: boy desires mother, fears father (castration anxiety). Girls: penis envy (Jung later called it the "Electra complex"). Resolved by identifying with same-sex parent → superego forms.

    Latency6–puberty

    Sexual drive is quiet; energy goes into school, friends, skills.

    Genitalpuberty on

    Mature adult sexuality and the ability to "love and work" (lieben und arbeiten).

    RememberO-A-P-L-G: "Old Aunties Play Ludo Gracefully."

    Therapy techniques and evaluation

    Techniques

    • Free association: say whatever comes to mind
    • Dream analysis: "the royal road to the unconscious"; manifest content (story) vs latent content (hidden meaning)
    • Transference: client puts feelings about parents onto the therapist
    • Resistance and Freudian slips (parapraxes)

    Evaluation

    • + First complete theory of personality; discovered the importance of the unconscious and childhood
    • + Defence mechanisms are widely accepted
    • − Hard to test scientifically (not falsifiable)
    • − Overemphasises sex; based on a few Viennese patients; biased views of women
    Analytical Psychology
    Carl Gustav Jung
    Lived
    1875–1961, born in Kesswil, Switzerland; worked in Zürich
    Link to Freud
    Freud's chosen "crown prince"; first president of the International Psychoanalytic Association; broke with Freud in 1913
    Key work
    Psychological Types (1921)
    Photo

    "Beneath your personal memories lies a collective unconscious shared by all humanity."

    Levels of the psyche

    Archetypes

    Archetypes are inborn images or patterns that appear in myths, dreams and religions all over the world.

    Persona

    The social mask we show the world. Problem if we over-identify with it.

    Shadow

    The dark, unwanted side of personality we don't admit. Appears as villains in dreams.

    Anima

    The feminine side of a man.

    Animus

    The masculine side of a woman.

    Great Mother

    Nourishing and destroying; fertility, Mother Earth.

    Wise Old Man

    Wisdom and meaning; the guru or prophet.

    Hero

    Overcomes a monster or evil, often with a weakness.

    Self

    The archetype of wholeness and balance; symbolised by the mandala (circle).

    Psychological types: 2 attitudes × 4 functions = 8 types

    Attitudes

    Introversion: energy turned inward, toward one's own thoughts. Extraversion: energy turned outward, toward people and objects. Everyone has both; one is dominant.

    Functions

    Rational: Thinking (what it means) and Feeling (its value). Irrational: Sensing (that it exists) and Intuiting (where it's going).

    FunctionExtravertedIntroverted
    ThinkingLogical about facts in the world (scientist)Logical about own ideas (philosopher)
    FeelingValues set by society; sociableDeep private values; quiet
    SensingEnjoys concrete experiences (food, sport)Rich inner reaction to sensations (artist)
    IntuitingSees possibilities in the world (entrepreneur)Inner visions (mystic, prophet)

    The modern Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is based on Jung's types.

    Development and methods

    Individuation (self-realization)

    The life-long process of becoming a whole person: accepting the shadow, balancing anima/animus, integrating opposites. It mostly happens in middle life (after about 35–40).

    Stages: childhood, youth, middle life, old age.

    Methods
    • Word association test (finds complexes)
    • Dream analysis (looks for archetypes)
    • Active imagination
    • Psychotherapy

    Jung used both causality (past) and teleology (future goals).

    Individual Psychology
    Alfred Adler
    Lived
    1870–1937, born near Vienna, Austria; died in Aberdeen, Scotland
    Childhood
    Sickly child (rickets, pneumonia); second son who felt overshadowed by his older brother
    Link to Freud
    Member of Freud's circle; left in 1911 to found Individual Psychology
    Key work
    The Practice and Theory of Individual Psychology (1927)
    Photo
    public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

    "To be human means to feel inferior, and to strive to overcome it."

    Inferiority → compensation → striving

    Inferiority complex: inferiority feelings so strong they block action. Superiority complex: an exaggerated show of superiority that hides inferiority.

    Six tenets of Individual Psychology

    1. Striving for success or superiority is the one driving force behind all behaviour.
    2. Subjective perceptions shape behaviour. Fictional finalism: we are pulled by imagined future goals (e.g. "If I become a doctor, I'll be respected").
    3. Unity of personality: the person is one consistent whole. Even body symptoms speak ("organ dialect").
    4. Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl): feeling of oneness with all humanity; the measure of mental health.
    5. Style of life: each person's unique way of striving, set by about age 4–5.
    6. Creative power: we are free to shape our own style of life from heredity and environment.
    RememberS-S-U-S-S-C: "Super Students Understand Social Style Creatively."

    Abnormal development and safeguarding tendencies

    Three childhood risks
    • Exaggerated physical deficiencies (organ inferiority)
    • Pampered style of life
    • Neglected style of life
    Safeguarding tendencies

    Conscious ways to protect self-esteem (unlike Freud's unconscious defences):

    • Excuses: "Yes, but…"
    • Aggression: depreciation, accusation, self-accusation
    • Withdrawal: moving backward, standing still, hesitating, constructing obstacles

    Masculine protest: Adler said society overvalues masculinity, so both men and women may strive to appear "manly" and strong.

    Birth order

    PositionPositive traitsNegative traits
    OldestResponsible, protective, organised"Dethroned" by the next child; anxious, may become hostile or rule-bound
    SecondMotivated, competitive, cooperativeRivalry; easily discouraged
    YoungestAmbitious, realisticPampered, dependent; inferiority feelings
    Only childSocially matureExaggerated superiority, low cooperation, pampered

    Adler also used early recollections (a person's earliest memories) to discover their style of life.

    "Neurosis comes from disturbed relationships and culture, not from biology."

    Basic hostility → basic anxiety

    If parents fail to give a child genuine warmth and affection (through domination, neglect, broken promises, unfairness), the child feels basic hostility toward them. But the child also depends on them, so the hostility is repressed.

    Repressed hostility leads to basic anxiety: "a feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world."

    Four ways people protect themselves: gaining affection, being submissive, attaining power/prestige/possession, and withdrawing.

    Three neurotic trends

    Normal vs neurotic: Healthy people use all three flexibly. Neurotic people are rigidly stuck in one, and the trends conflict.

    Intrapsychic conflicts and feminine psychology

    Idealized self-image

    Neurotics build a glorified picture of who they "should" be. This leads to the neurotic search for glory (need for perfection, neurotic ambition, vindictive triumph), neurotic claims and neurotic pride.

    Horney called this the "tyranny of the should".

    Self-hatred

    When the real self can't match the ideal self, the person hates the real self: relentless demands on self, merciless self-accusation, self-contempt, self-frustration, self-torment, self-destructive actions.

    Feminine psychology

    Rejected Freud's "penis envy". Women's feelings of inferiority come from culture and social limits, not anatomy. She suggested men may feel "womb envy".

    Therapy

    Goal: help the client give up the idealized self and grow toward the real self. Horney believed in self-analysis.

    Key ideas

    Ego is central

    Erikson's is an ego psychology: the ego is a positive, active force, not just a servant of the id. Three facets: body ego, ego ideal, ego identity.

    Epigenetic principle

    Like a growing plant, each stage emerges from and builds on the earlier one, on a timetable set by biology and society.

    Psychosocial crisis

    Each stage has a conflict between a syntonic (positive) and a dystonic (negative) quality. A healthy balance (more positive) gives a basic strength or virtue.

    Core pathology

    Too little basic strength gives a core pathology for that stage (e.g. withdrawal instead of hope).

    Lived
    1893–1988, born in New York City, USA
    Career

    "Behaviour comes from the meeting of inner needs and outside press."

    Personology and Murray's view of personality

    Murray called the study of the whole person over a lifetime personology. He used Freud's id, ego and superego but changed them. For Murray the id also contains positive impulses (love, curiosity), and the ego actively organises behaviour.

    Personality is rooted in the brain, reduces tension, and is always changing over time.

    Needs and press → thema

    Need

    A force in the brain that organises perception, thinking and action to change an unsatisfying situation. It produces tension; satisfying it reduces tension.

    Press

    Environmental forces that help or block a need. Alpha press: the environment as it really is. Beta press: the environment as the person perceives it. Beta press matters more.

    Types of needs

    PairMeaning
    Primary vs secondaryPrimary (viscerogenic): bodily, e.g. food, water, air. Secondary (psychogenic): learned, psychological, e.g. achievement.
    Reactive vs proactiveReactive: response to something in the environment. Proactive: arises from within.
    Overt vs covertOvert: freely expressed. Covert: hidden, shown in fantasy or dreams.
    Focal vs diffuseFocal: tied to a specific object. Diffuse: general.
    Effect vs modalEffect: aims at a goal. Modal: enjoyed for its own sake (e.g. music).

    How needs interact: prepotency (an urgent need comes first, e.g. hunger), fusion (one act satisfies two needs), subsidiation (one need serves another), contrafactuation (a need appears as its opposite).

    The 20 psychogenic needs (Explorations in Personality, 1938)

    GroupNeeds

    Defence of statusDefendance (to defend self against blame) · Infavoidance (to avoid humiliation) · Harmavoidance (to avoid pain, danger)
    PowerDominance (to control others) · Deference (to admire, follow a superior) · Autonomy (to be free) · Aggression (to attack, overcome) · Abasement (to submit, accept blame)
    AffectionAffiliation (to be with others) · Rejection (to exclude others) · Nurturance (to help, protect) · Succorance (to be helped) · Play (to have fun) · Sex

    Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

    What it is

    A projective test developed with Christiana Morgan (1935). The set has 31 cards (30 ambiguous pictures + 1 blank). The person tells a story about each: what led up to it, what is happening, what people think and feel, how it ends.

    Why it works

    People project their own needs and press into the stories. Repeated themes reveal the person's main needs. McClelland later used it to measure need for achievement (n-Ach).

    Evaluation: + rich description of motivation; TAT widely used. − long list of needs is hard to test; TAT scoring is subjective.

    Existential Psychology
    Rollo May
    Lived
    1909–1994, born in Ada, Ohio, USA
    Life
    Studied divinity (influenced by theologian Paul Tillich); survived tuberculosis in the 1940s, which shaped his ideas on anxiety; PhD in clinical psychology, Columbia (1949)

    "We are free to choose, and anxiety is the price of that freedom."

    Background: existentialism

    Existentialism (Kierkegaard, Heidegger, Sartre) says: existence comes before essence. What we do matters more than what we are by nature. People are free and responsible for who they become. May brought these ideas into psychology in America.

    Being-in-the-world (Dasein) and its three modes

    We are a unity of person and world. Problems arise when we feel cut off from the world, ourselves or others: alienation.

    RememberUmwelt = Universe around · Mitwelt = Mates · Eigenwelt = Ego/self

    Nonbeing, anxiety and guilt

    Nonbeing

    Awareness that we will die someday. It can push us to live fully, or to escape into conformity.

    Normal anxiety

    In proportion to the threat; not repressed; can be used constructively. It is part of growth and freedom.

    Neurotic anxiety

    Out of proportion to the threat; involves repression; blocks growth.

    Ontological guilt

    Guilt from failing to fulfil our potential. Three forms, one per mode: separation from nature (Umwelt), inability to understand others (Mitwelt), denial of our own potential (Eigenwelt).

    Intentionality, care, love and will

    Intentionality links subject and object: the structure that gives meaning to experience and lets us make decisions about the future. Care means recognising something matters. Love and will both depend on care.

    Will is the ability to organise oneself to move toward a goal. Love and will are united in healthy people but are often split in modern society.

    Four forms of love

    Sex

    Biological drive; tension release.

    Eros

    Psychological desire for lasting union; creative, "making love".

    Philia

    Intimate, non-sexual friendship; takes time to grow.

    Agape

    Unselfish love for others without expecting anything back (altruism).

    RememberS-E-P-A: "Some Exams Pass Automatically." (Sex, Eros, Philia, Agape)

    Freedom, destiny, myths and psychopathology

    Freedom

    Existential freedom: freedom of doing (action). Essential freedom: freedom of being (inner freedom, even in prison).

    Destiny

    Our limits: death, gender, heredity, culture. Freedom and destiny are paradoxes that need each other.

    Power of myth

    Myths (e.g. Oedipus) are shared stories that give meaning. Modern people suffer from a lack of myths.

    Psychopathology

    Apathy and emptiness from alienation. Therapy aims to make people free and aware, not just remove symptoms.

    Evaluation: + deep insight into anxiety, love and freedom; rich for therapy. − concepts are vague, philosophical and hard to test scientifically.

    Gordon W. Allport
    Lived
    1897–1967, born in Montezuma, Indiana, USA; spent most of his career at Harvard
    Famous story

    "Every person is unique, and psychology should study the individual."

    Personal dispositions: three levels

    Allport separated common traits (shared by many people; used to compare) from personal dispositions (unique to one person). Personal dispositions come in three levels:

    Dispositions can also be motivational (they drive us, e.g. ambition) or stylistic (how we do things, e.g. politeness).

    The proprium

    Allport avoided the word "self" and used proprium: everything that is "peculiarly ours", warm and central to our identity. It develops through 7 aspects:

    1. Bodily self: sense of one's own body (infancy)
    2. Self-identity: "I am the same person" over time
    3. Self-esteem: pride in doing things
    4. Extension of self: "my toy, my family, my country"
    5. Self-image: how others see me; who I should be
    6. Rational coper: using reason to solve problems
    7. Propriate striving: long-term goals that give life direction

    Functional autonomy

    Adult motives become independent of their original childhood reasons. A boy may first play cricket to please his father, but as an adult he plays for its own sake.

    Perseverative functional autonomy

    Low-level habits that keep going: e.g. always waking at 5 am even after retirement.

    Propriate functional autonomy

    Self-sustaining motives tied to the proprium: values, interests, life goals.

    The mature (healthy) personality: 6 criteria

    1. Extension of the sense of self: involved in activities beyond oneself

    2. Warm relating to others: intimacy and compassion

    3. Emotional security: self-acceptance, frustration tolerance

    4. Realistic perception: sees the world as it is

    5. Insight and humour: self-objectification, can laugh at oneself

    6. Unifying philosophy of life: clear purpose and values

    Remember"Every Warm Emotionally Ready Indian Unites." (Extension, Warmth, Emotional security, Realism, Insight/humour, Unifying philosophy)

    Methods and evaluation

    Idiographic vs nomothetic

    Idiographic (Allport preferred; he later called it morphogenic): in-depth study of one individual. Nomothetic: comparing large groups on common traits.

    Letters from Jenny: analysis of 301 letters written by one woman.

    Evaluation

    • + Stressed uniqueness and the healthy, conscious adult
    • + Trait-word study started the lexical approach (→ Cattell, Big Five)
    • − Personal dispositions are hard to study scientifically
    • − Functional autonomy explains little about how motives change
    Biologically based factor theory
    Hans J. Eysenck
    Lived
    1916–1997, born in Berlin, Germany; left for England in 1934 to escape the Nazis
    Career
    Institute of Psychiatry, Maudsley Hospital, London
    Key works

    "Personality comes down to three biological dimensions: P, E and N."

    Hierarchy of behaviour organisation

    Four criteria for accepting a factor: (1) psychometric evidence (factor analysis), (2) heritability, (3) makes theoretical sense, (4) social relevance.

    The three superfactors (PEN)

    E · Extraversion–Introversion

    Sociable, lively, active, assertive, sensation-seeking vs quiet, reserved.

    Basis: cortical arousal via the ARAS. Introverts have higher natural arousal, so they avoid extra stimulation; extraverts seek it.

    N · Neuroticism–Stability

    Anxious, moody, tense, guilty, low self-esteem vs calm, even-tempered.

    Basis: reactivity of the limbic system ("visceral brain").

    P · Psychoticism–Superego

    Aggressive, cold, egocentric, impulsive, antisocial, tough-minded, but also creative.

    Basis: linked to male hormones (testosterone) and low MAO enzyme.

    RememberPEN: Eysenck writes personality with a PEN.

    E × N and the four ancient temperaments

    Eysenck linked his E and N dimensions to the four temperaments of Hippocrates and Galen.

    Measurement and evaluation

    Questionnaires
    • MPI (Maudsley Personality Inventory, 1959): E, N
    • EPI (Eysenck Personality Inventory, 1964): E, N + Lie scale
    • EPQ (1975): P, E, N + Lie scale; EPQ-R later

    Classic finding: introverts produce more saliva when lemon juice is put on the tongue (higher arousal).

    Evaluation

    • + Simple, testable, biologically grounded, strong research support
    • + E and N appear in nearly every trait model
    • − P is the weakest, poorly named factor
    • − Three factors may be too few (cf. Big Five)

    Dynamic traits and the specification equation

    Ergs

    Innate drives: hunger, sex, fear, curiosity, gregariousness.

    Sentiments

    Learned attitudes toward people, objects or institutions. The most important is the self-sentiment.

    Attitudes serve sentiments, which serve ergs. Cattell drew this as the dynamic lattice (subsidiation).

    Evaluation: + highly scientific, data-based; 16PF widely used in counselling and selection. − results depend on which data enter the factor analysis; 16 factors hard to replicate exactly.

    Five-Factor Theory (FFT)

    Basic tendencies

    The five traits; biologically based, not shaped by environment.

    Characteristic adaptations

    Acquired habits, attitudes, skills and relationships that change with culture and experience.

    Self-concept

    A characteristic adaptation: our knowledge and views of ourselves.

    Key claims

    • Universality: the five factors appear in many cultures and languages, studied in about 50 cultures.
    • Stability: traits are fairly stable after about age 30.
    • Heritability: twin studies show each factor is substantially inherited.

    Evaluation: + strong cross-cultural and research support; useful in clinical, organisational and health settings. − describes more than it explains; five may miss traits (e.g. honesty–humility in the HEXACO model); relies mostly on self-report.

    Cognitive-Behavioural Approaches

    The 5-minute summary

    What does "cognitive-behavioural" mean? Freud looked for hidden unconscious drives. Behaviourists like Skinner looked only at rewards and punishments. The cognitive-behavioural theorists sit in the middle: behaviour is learned in social situations, but what decides it is what goes on in the person's mind. That means their expectations, beliefs, values and interpretations.

    Rotter

    "What do I expect, and how much do I want it?"

    Behaviour = Expectancy + Reinforcement value, in a psychological situation. Famous for locus of control.

    Mischel

    "It depends on the situation."

    People are consistent in their if…then patterns, not across all situations. CAPS model and the marshmallow test.

    Kelly

    "Every person is a scientist."

    We understand the world through personal constructs (bipolar categories like kind–cruel). Rep Test and fixed-role therapy.

    "Behaviour depends on what you expect to happen and how much you value it."

    Five basic assumptions

    1. Humans interact with their meaningful environment. Both the person and the situation matter.
    2. Human personality is learned. It can be changed at any age through new experience.
    3. Personality has a basic unity. Past experience gives some stability.
    4. Motivation is goal-directed. People seek rewards (reinforcements) and avoid punishment.
    5. People can anticipate events. We think ahead about what will happen.

    Predicting a specific behaviour: the 4 variables

    BP · Behaviour Potential

    How likely a particular behaviour is to happen in a given situation.

    Example: How likely is Riya to study tonight?

    E · Expectancy

    The person's own estimate (subjective probability) that a behaviour will bring a reward. It can be specific (E′) or generalised (GE).

    "If I study, I'll probably pass."

    RV · Reinforcement Value

    How much a person prefers one reward over others, when the chances of getting each are equal.

    How much does Riya care about good marks?

    s · Psychological Situation

    The situation as the person sees it, not as it objectively is.

    A unit test may feel "important" to one student and "trivial" to another.

    RememberB-E-R-S: "Behaviour Expects Reward in a Situation."
    BPx,s1,Ra = f ( Ex,Ra,s1 & RVa,s1 )

    Read it as: the potential for behaviour x in situation 1, with reinforcement a, is a function of the expectancy that x will bring a in that situation, and the value of a in that situation.

    Predicting general behaviour: needs

    For broad, long-term patterns Rotter used needs. Needs are groups of behaviours aimed at the same goal.

    NP · Need Potential

    How likely a set of related behaviours is to occur. It is the broad version of BP.

    FM · Freedom of Movement

    The person's overall expectation of success at meeting a need. It is the broad version of E.

    NV · Need Value

    How much the person prefers one set of rewards over another. It is the broad version of RV.

    NP = f ( FM & NV )

    Locus of control (Internal–External Control of Reinforcement)

    This is Rotter's most famous idea, a generalised expectancy. It is measured by the I–E Scale (1966): 29 forced-choice items, of which 23 are scored and 6 are fillers.

    Internal locus

    "What happens to me depends on my own effort and ability."

    External locus

    Maladaptive behaviour

    Any persistent behaviour that fails to move a person closer to their goal. It usually comes from high need value + low freedom of movement: wanting something badly but expecting to fail.

    Psychotherapy

    The therapist acts as an active teacher with two aims: (1) changing goals that are unrealistic, and (2) eliminating low expectancies of success.

    "Ask not only what kind of person someone is, but in which situations they behave that way."

    Background: the consistency paradox

    Most people believe personality is consistent: an honest person is honest everywhere. But when Mischel reviewed the research in 1968, he found that behaviour in one situation predicted behaviour in another quite weakly. The correlations were usually no higher than about r = .30, which he called the "personality coefficient".

    This is the consistency paradox: we feel people are consistent, yet the data shows they vary a lot across situations. It started the famous person–situation debate.

    Person–situation interaction: "if…then" behavioural signatures

    Mischel and Shoda's answer: people are consistent, but in a stable if…then pattern. If situation A, then I do X; if situation B, then I do Y. This stable pattern is a person's behavioural signature of personality.

    CAPS: the Cognitive-Affective Personality System

    Personality is a stable system of mental units that process information about situations. Different situations "switch on" different units, which is why behaviour varies but the pattern stays stable.

    The 5 cognitive-affective units

    1 · Encoding strategies

    How a person interprets a situation or person.

    Is a friend's silence "anger" or "tiredness"?

    2 · Competencies & self-regulatory strategies

    What a person can do, and how they control their own behaviour through self-set goals and self-rewards.

    3 · Expectancies & beliefs

    Beliefs about what will happen if I act (behaviour–outcome) and whether I can act (self-efficacy).

    4 · Goals & values

    What the person wants and finds important. These guide long-term plans.

    5 · Affective responses

    Emotions and feelings (and physical reactions) that colour every other unit.

    RememberE-C-E-G-A: "Every Child Expects Good Apples."
    Behaviour prediction: "If personality is a stable system that processes information about situations, then individuals encountering different situations should behave differently across situations."

    Situation variables: Mischel also stressed that some situations are so strong (a red traffic light, an exam hall) that almost everyone behaves alike. Personality shows most in weak, ambiguous situations.

    Delay of gratification: the marshmallow test

    In the late 1960s at Stanford's Bing Nursery School, children aged about 4 were offered a choice: one treat now, or two treats if they could wait alone (about 15 minutes) until the researcher returned.

    Follow-up studies found that children who waited longer tended, years later, to have higher SAT scores and better coping skills. Later replications (2018) found this link was smaller than first reported once family background was taken into account. That is a good point for "critical evaluation" answers.

    "Hot" system (go)

    Emotional, fast, impulsive. Focuses on the tempting features: "sweet, soft, yummy."

    Strengthened by looking at the treat.

    "Cool" system (know)

    Thinking, slow, strategic. Self-control strategies:

    • Distraction (singing, playing)
    • Covering or looking away from the treat
    • Thinking of it "coolly": "it's just a fluffy cloud"

    Key point: Self-control is a learnable cognitive skill, not just a fixed trait. This fits the self-regulatory strategies unit of CAPS.

    "Every person is a scientist who builds theories about the world and tests them."

    Kelly's philosophical position

    Person as scientist

    Ordinary people, like scientists, try to predict and control events. They form hypotheses, test them through behaviour, and revise them.

    Scientist as person

    Scientists are ordinary people too. Their theories are just their own personal constructions of events.

    Constructive alternativism

    There is always more than one way to interpret any event. Nobody is a victim of their past; we can always choose to reconstrue it.

    Personal constructs

    A personal construct is a person's own way of seeing how some things are alike and at the same time different from others. It is like a pair of "glasses" or a template we fit over reality. Every construct has two opposite poles.

    Bipolar construct

    Kind ↔ Selfish

    One person might use "Kind ↔ Strict" instead; the opposite pole is personal.

    Range of convenience

    Each construct works only for some events.

    "Tall ↔ short" works for people and trees, not for emotions.

    Permeability

    A permeable construct can take in new events. An impermeable one is rigid and closed.

    Fundamental postulate + 11 corollaries

    Fundamental postulate: "A person's processes are psychologically channelized by the ways in which he anticipates events."

    In simple words: our thinking and behaviour are guided by how we predict the future. The 11 corollaries explain this postulate.

    1. Construction: We anticipate events by noticing what repeats (replications).
    2. Individuality: People differ in how they construe the same events.
    3. Organization: Constructs form a hierarchy; some are superordinate, some subordinate.
    4. Dichotomy: Every construct is bipolar (two opposite poles).
    5. Choice: We choose the pole that best extends and defines our system.
    6. Range: Each construct applies to a limited range of events.
    7. Experience: Our construct system changes as we meet new experiences.
    8. Modulation: How much the system can change depends on how permeable the constructs are.
    9. Fragmentation: We can hold sub-systems that contradict each other.
    10. Commonality: People who construe events similarly will think and act similarly.
    11. Sociality: To relate to someone, we must understand how they construe things.
    Remember"Can I Organize Difficult Choices? Real Experience Mends Fragmented Common Sense."

    Abnormal development: four key emotions

    Kelly defined unpleasant emotions in terms of constructs, not drives. Disorder means using constructs that keep failing, but not changing them.

    Threat

    Awareness that our core constructs are about to change in a big, comprehensive way.

    e.g. Facing one's own death.

    Fear

    A specific, incidental new construct is about to take over. It is narrower than threat.

    Anxiety

    Recognising that events lie outside the range of convenience of our construct system. We cannot make sense of what is happening.

    Guilt

    A sense of having lost one's core role structure: acting against the role one believes defines oneself.

    Psychotherapy and assessment

    Fixed-role therapy

    The therapist writes a "fixed-role sketch" of a new character who construes life differently. The client acts out this role for about 2 weeks, as if it were an experiment. The aim is to show the client that their constructs can be changed (constructive alternativism in action).

    Rep Test (Role Construct Repertory Test)

    The client lists important people (role titles: self, mother, a liked teacher, a disliked person…). The tester picks three at a time and asks: "How are two of these alike, and different from the third?" The answers reveal the client's personal constructs.

    Strengths

    • Testable and research-based (I–E scale, CAPS studies, Rep grid)

    Advanced Social Psychology · Unit I

    Introduction and Prosocial Behaviour

    What is social psychology?

    Social psychology studies how other people, whether real, imagined or implied, affect the way we think, feel and behave. It looks at the individual in a social situation, while sociology looks at groups and society as a whole.

    Gordon Allport (1954/1985)

    "An attempt to understand and explain how the thoughts, feelings and behaviour of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined or implied presence of others."

    The classic definition. Learn it word for word.

    Baron & Branscombe

    "The scientific field that seeks to understand the nature and causes of individual behaviour, feelings and thought in social situations."

    Four key features (from the definition)

    Scientific

    Uses the scientific method: experiments, surveys, observation. It values accuracy, objectivity, scepticism and open-mindedness.

    Focus on the individual

    Studies how one person is affected, not whole societies.

    Nature and causes

    Not just describing behaviour but explaining why it happens.

    Social situations

    Other people, even imagined ones, change our behaviour.

    Where social psychology sits

    Scope: what it studies

    Social thinking

    Social cognition, perception, attribution, the self, attitudes

    Social influence

    Conformity, compliance, obedience, persuasion

    Social relations

    Prejudice, aggression, helping, attraction, love

    Groups

    Group processes, leadership, decision-making, conflict

    Applied areas

    Health, law, workplace, environment, education, politics

    RememberScope = Thinking, Influence, Relations (+ groups and applications): "how we think about, are influenced by, and relate to one another" (David Myers).

    Importance

    • Understanding everyday life: why we help, conform, fall in love or hate.
    • Solving social problems: reducing prejudice, violence, communal conflict and bullying.
    • Health: changing health habits (e.g. hand-washing, vaccination campaigns).
    • Law: eyewitness accuracy, jury decisions.
    • Workplace: teamwork, leadership, motivation.
    • Self-understanding: knowing our own biases helps us think more fairly.

    Prosocial behaviour

    Prosocial behaviour is any act that benefits others, such as helping, sharing, donating, cooperating or volunteering. It may or may not cost the helper something, and it may or may not have selfish motives.

    Prosocial behaviour

    The widest term: any helpful act, whatever the motive.

    Helping

    An act that intentionally benefits another person.

    Altruism

    Helping purely for the other's benefit, often at a cost to oneself, with no expectation of reward.

    The bystander effect

    Real event · 1964

    Kitty Genovese, New York

    Newspapers reported that 38 neighbours witnessed her murder and none helped. Later research found the number and details were exaggerated, but the case inspired the research below.

    L&D
    Bibb Latané & John Darley (1968)Students heard another "participant" have a seizure over an intercom. Alone: about 85% helped quickly. With 4 others believed present: about 31% helped.

    Bystander effect: the more bystanders present, the less likely any one person is to help. The main reason is diffusion of responsibility: "someone else will do it."

    Five steps to helping (Latané & Darley)

    RememberN-I-A-K-D: "Never Ignore Anyone; Kindly Do help."

    Empathy

    Empathy is the ability to feel and understand another person's emotional state, to "put yourself in their shoes".

    Affective (emotional) empathy

    Feeling what the other person feels: their sadness makes you sad.

    Cognitive empathy (perspective-taking)

    Understanding what they think and feel, even without feeling it.

    Empathic concern

    Feeling sympathy and wanting to help. This is the part that most strongly predicts helping.

    Empathy shows early (babies cry when other babies cry), increases with age, and is partly inherited (twin studies) and partly learned from parents.

    Why do we help? Four explanations

    CB
    C. Daniel BatsonEmpathy-altruism hypothesis (1981 onwards): true altruism exists.
    1 · Empathy-altruism hypothesis (Batson)

    When we feel empathic concern, we help purely to relieve the other person's distress, even when we could easily escape.

    2 · Negative-state relief model (Cialdini)

    We help to relieve our own bad mood: seeing suffering makes us feel bad, and helping makes us feel better. Selfish motive.

    3 · Empathic joy hypothesis

    We help because we enjoy seeing the positive effect of our help on the other person.

    4 · Genetic determinism (kin selection)

    Evolution favours helping genetic relatives, since it helps our genes survive. We help family more than strangers.

    Determinants of prosocial behaviour

    What makes helping more or less likely? Group the factors into situational, personal and emotional.

    A. Situational factors

    Number of bystanders

    More bystanders → less helping (diffusion of responsibility).

    Ambiguity

    If it's unclear whether it's an emergency, people hold back.

    Time pressure

    Good Samaritan study (Darley & Batson, 1973): seminary students in a hurry rarely stopped to help a groaning man, even on the way to give a talk on the Good Samaritan.

    Similarity and liking

    We help people who are like us, attractive, or part of our in-group.

    Deservingness

    We help more if the victim is not responsible for their own problem.

    Role models

    Seeing others help increases helping.

    Place

    People in villages and small towns help strangers more than people in big crowded cities.

    B. Personal factors

    Altruistic personality

    High empathy, belief in a just world, social responsibility, internal locus of control, low egocentrism.

    Skills and competence

    People with relevant training (e.g. first aid) help more.

    Religion and values

    Values such as seva and dana encourage helping, especially when they are internalised.

    Gender

    Men help more in risky, heroic situations; women give more long-term, nurturing help.

    Culture

    Collectivist cultures help in-group members more strongly.

    C. Emotional factors

    Good mood

    Usually increases helping (feel good, do good), unless helping would spoil the mood.

    Bad mood

    Guilt often increases helping. Sadness can increase helping if helping lifts the mood (negative-state relief).

    RememberDeterminants = S-P-E: Situation, Person, Emotion.

    How to increase helping

    • Reduce ambiguity: say clearly "This is an emergency!"
    • Assign responsibility: point to one person ("You in the blue shirt, call 108").
    • Teach skills (first aid) and model helping.
    • Build empathy and a shared identity ("we are all one community").

    Advanced Social Psychology · Unit II

    Social Cognition and Social Perception

    What is social cognition?

    Social cognition is how we interpret, analyse, remember and use information about the social world: about ourselves, other people and situations.

    We have limited mental capacity, so we are "cognitive misers": we save effort whenever we can. So there are two modes of thinking.

    Low-effort (automatic) thinking

    Schemas

    A schema is a mental framework that organises our knowledge about a topic and guides how we process new information.

    Person schemas

    About types of people: "a typical professor".

    Role schemas

    About social roles: "doctor", "mother".

    Event schemas (scripts)

    About what happens in events: a restaurant visit, a wedding.

    Self-schemas

    About ourselves: "I am good at maths".

    Schemas affect three stages of processing

    Attention

    We notice what fits (or sharply violates) the schema.

    Encoding

    What we notice gets stored in memory.

    Retrieval

    We remember schema-consistent information better.

    Priming

    Recent experiences make a schema easier to activate. Seeing words about rudeness makes people interrupt more (Bargh et al., 1996).

    Perseverance effect

    Schemas resist change, even when the evidence for them is shown to be false.

    Key study · 1968

    Self-fulfilling prophecy: "Pygmalion in the classroom"

    Rosenthal & Jacobson told teachers that some randomly chosen pupils were "late bloomers". By the end of the year those pupils showed bigger IQ gains: the teachers' expectations (schemas) changed how they treated them.

    Amos Tversky & Daniel Kahneman (1974)Showed that people rely on simple rules of thumb that are quick but cause predictable errors. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics.Photo: nrkbeta, CC BY-SA 2.0, Wikimedia Commons
    Representativeness

    Judging by how similar someone is to a typical member of a category, while ignoring base rates.

    "He is quiet and wears glasses, so he must be a librarian, not a farmer."

    Availability

    Judging how common something is by how easily examples come to mind.

    After news of a plane crash, flying feels more dangerous than driving.

    Anchoring and adjustment

    Starting from a reference point (anchor) and adjusting too little.

    A shop's "original price ₹2,000" makes ₹1,200 seem cheap.

    Status quo heuristic

    Assuming the existing option is better than a new one.

    RememberR-A-A: "Really Available Anchors." (Representativeness, Availability, Anchoring)

    Types of automatic thinking

    • Automatic priming: schemas activated without our awareness.
    • Automatic stereotyping: categorising people by race, gender, age in milliseconds.
    • Automatic affect: instant liking or disliking ("affect is primary", Zajonc).
    • Implicit attitudes: evaluations we may not consciously endorse (see Unit IV).

    High-effort (controlled) thinking

    Controlled thinking is conscious, deliberate and effortful. We use it when the stakes are high, we have time, or something unexpected happens. But it also has typical errors.

    Counterfactual thinking

    Imagining "what might have been". Upward ("if only I'd studied more") gives regret but motivates. Downward ("it could have been worse") brings relief.

    Bronze medallists often look happier than silver medallists (Medvec et al., 1995).

    Planning fallacy

    Underestimating how long a task will take. Students almost always start revising too late.

    Optimistic bias

    Expecting good things to happen to us more than to others.

    Negativity bias

    Negative information gets more attention and weight than positive.

    Thought suppression

    Trying hard not to think of something can make it come back more often ("don't think of a white bear", Wegner).

    Magical thinking

    Beliefs with no rational basis: "if I wear my lucky pen, I'll pass".

    Affect and cognition

    Feelings and thinking influence each other. Good moods make us rely more on heuristics and stereotypes; bad moods make us more careful. Our thoughts also shape our feelings (e.g. how we label an arousal).

    Nonverbal communication

    Social perception is how we try to understand other people. Our first clue is nonverbal communication: communication without words.

    Facial expressions

    Six basic emotions recognised across cultures: happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust (Paul Ekman). Contempt is sometimes added.

    Eye contact (gaze)

    Shows liking and attention; a long stare can signal hostility.

    Body language

    Posture, gestures, movements. Emblems are gestures with specific meanings (thumbs-up, namaste).

    Touch

    Can show warmth, comfort, dominance; meaning depends on culture and relationship.

    Paralanguage

    Tone, pitch, speed and loudness of voice.

    Personal space

    Distance we keep shows intimacy and status.

    Paul EkmanShowed basic facial expressions are universal: even the isolated Fore people of Papua New Guinea recognised them.Photo: Paul Ekman Group, CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons
    RememberSix basic emotions: "Happy Sad Angry Friends Surprise Dad." (Happiness, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Surprise, Disgust)

    Detecting deception

    People are only slightly better than chance at spotting lies. Useful cues include micro-expressions (flashes of emotion lasting a fraction of a second), interchannel discrepancies (face says one thing, voice another), changes in pitch, and exaggerated or very brief eye contact.

    First impressions

    We form impressions of people very quickly, sometimes from a face shown for just 100 milliseconds (Willis & Todorov, 2006), and they tend to last.

    SA
    Solomon Asch (1946)Showed that some traits are "central" and change the whole impression.
    Key study · Asch, 1946

    Warm vs cold: central traits

    Two groups read the same list of traits (intelligent, skilful, industrious, warm / cold, determined, practical, cautious). Only one word differed. The "warm" person was seen as generous, happy and good-natured; the "cold" person very differently.

    Primacy effect

    Information we receive first has more influence. "Intelligent, industrious… envious" is liked more than the same list in reverse order (Asch).

    Implicit personality theories

    Our beliefs about which traits go together, e.g. "beautiful people are also kind" (halo effect).

    Impression management

    We try to control the impressions others form of us (self-presentation).

    Self-enhancement

    Making ourselves look good: dress, grooming, highlighting achievements.

    Other-enhancement (ingratiation)

    Making the other person feel good: flattery, agreeing, showing interest.

    Causal attribution

    Attribution is how we explain the causes of our own and others' behaviour.

    FH
    Fritz Heider (1958)Father of attribution theory. People are "naive scientists" explaining behaviour by internal (dispositional) or external (situational) causes.
    Internal attribution

    Cause is inside the person: personality, effort, ability. "She failed because she's lazy."

    External attribution

    Cause is in the situation: luck, task difficulty, other people. "She failed because the paper was hard."

    Jones & Davis: correspondent inference (1965)

    When do we infer that behaviour reflects a stable trait? When the behaviour is freely chosen, has non-common effects (a unique outcome only that choice gives), and is low in social desirability (not what anyone would do).

    Kelley's covariation model (1967)

    Consensus

    Do other people act the same way toward this stimulus?

    Consistency

    Does this person act the same way toward this stimulus at other times?

    Distinctiveness

    Does this person act this way toward other stimuli too?

    Attribution errors and biases

    Fundamental attribution error

    Over-blaming internal causes for others' behaviour and ignoring the situation (Ross, 1977). Also called correspondence bias.

    Actor–observer effect

    We explain our own behaviour by the situation but others' by their personality.

    Self-serving bias

    Success → internal ("I'm smart"); failure → external ("bad luck").

    Weiner added that attributions also differ in stability (stable vs unstable) and controllability, which affect motivation after success or failure.

    The Self, Cognitive Dissonance and Aggression

    The self-concept

    The self-concept is the organised set of beliefs we hold about who we are: our traits, roles, abilities, values and social identities.

    William James (1890)

    Split the self into the "I" (the self as knower, the one who thinks) and the "me" (the self as known: material, social and spiritual selves).

    Self-schema

    Our self-concept works like a schema: we notice and remember self-relevant information better (the self-reference effect).

    Origins: where does the self-concept come from?

    Looking-glass self

    Charles Cooley (1902): we see ourselves as we think others see us, like a mirror.

    Symbolic interactionism

    G. H. Mead: the self develops through social interaction and language.

    Self-recognition

    Mirror test: children recognise themselves at about 18–24 months.

    Social roles and identities

    Student, daughter, Indian, musician: each adds to the self.

    Culture

    Shapes whether the self is independent or interdependent (below).

    Culture and the self (Markus & Kitayama, 1991)

    Independent: "I am confident, creative". Interdependent: "I am a good son, a loyal friend". The self is defined by relationships.

    Ways of knowing oneself

    1 · Introspection

    Looking inward at our own thoughts and feelings. Limited: we often don't know why we do things, and we may invent reasons (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977).

    2 · Self-perception theory (Bem, 1972)

    When our inner feelings are unclear, we infer them by observing our own behaviour, like an outsider. "I go to the gym daily, so I must like fitness."

    3 · Social comparison (Festinger, 1954)

    We judge our abilities and opinions by comparing with others. Upward comparison (with better) can inspire or depress; downward (with worse) boosts self-esteem.

    4 · Feedback from others

    Others' reactions (the looking-glass self) and what close people tell us.

    Overjustification effect

    Self-perception also explains why rewarding an enjoyable activity can reduce intrinsic interest: "I'm doing it for the reward, not because I like it" (Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973).

    RememberI-S-S-F: Introspection, Self-perception, Social comparison, Feedback.

    Self-control

    Self-control (self-regulation) is the ability to override impulses and act toward long-term goals.

    Ego depletion (Baumeister)

    Self-control may work like a muscle that gets tired with use: after resisting cookies, people gave up sooner on puzzles. Later large studies found this effect much weaker than first reported, so mention it with caution.

    Strategies that help
    • Avoid temptation (remove the phone)
    • Implementation intentions: "If X, then I will do Y"
    • Distraction and "cool" thinking (Mischel)
    • Rest, sleep, glucose, positive mood

    Self-esteem

    Self-esteem is our overall evaluation of ourselves: the attitude we hold toward ourselves, positive or negative.

    Measurement

    Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (10 items) for explicit self-esteem; the Implicit Association Test for implicit self-esteem.

    Sources

    Feedback from others, social comparison, success in areas that matter to us.

    Sociometer theory

    Self-esteem is a gauge of how accepted and included we are (Leary).

    High self-esteem

    • Happier, more persistent after failure
    • Less depression
    • But very high, unstable self-esteem can become narcissism and aggression when threatened

    Low self-esteem

    • Linked with depression, anxiety
    • More sensitive to rejection
    • Self-handicapping (making excuses in advance)

    Self-esteem tends to drop in adolescence (especially for girls), rise through adulthood, and fall again in old age.

    LF
    Leon Festinger (1919–1989)American social psychologist. A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance (1957). Also proposed social comparison theory (1954).

    Cognitive dissonance

    Cognitive dissonance is the unpleasant tension we feel when we hold two inconsistent cognitions, or when our behaviour contradicts our attitudes.

    Because it feels bad, we are motivated to reduce it, often by changing our attitude to fit what we did.

    RememberReduce dissonance with B-A-C-T: change Behaviour, change Attitude, add Consonant thoughts, Trivialise.

    The $1 / $20 experiment (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959)

    Students did a very boring task (turning pegs) for an hour. They were then paid $1 or $20 to tell the next person it was fun. Later they rated how enjoyable the task really was (scale −5 to +5).

    Other forms of dissonance

    Induced compliance

    Being led to act against our attitude with little reward (the $1 study). Less reward → more attitude change.

    Effort justification

    We value what we suffered for. Aronson & Mills (1959): a severe initiation made a dull group discussion seem more interesting.

    Post-decision dissonance

    After choosing, we rate the chosen option higher and the rejected one lower ("spreading of alternatives", Brehm, 1956).

    Hypocrisy induction

    Reminding people that they preach something they don't practise makes them change behaviour (e.g. condom use, water saving).

    Alternative view: Bem's self-perception theory explains the $1 result without any tension: people simply infer their attitude from their behaviour.

    Aggression

    Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another person who wants to avoid that harm. Accidental harm is not aggression; an intended but failed attack is.

    Hostile (reactive) aggression

    Driven by anger; harm is the goal. "He insulted me, so I hit him."

    Instrumental aggression

    Harm is a means to another goal: robbery, a contract killing.

    Direct vs indirect

    Direct: physical or verbal attack. Indirect (relational): gossip, exclusion, spreading rumours.

    Views on aggression

    1 · Instinct theories

    Freud: Thanatos, the death instinct, turned outward. Konrad Lorenz: a fighting instinct that evolved for survival.

    2 · Biological and evolutionary

    Testosterone, low serotonin, brain areas (amygdala, prefrontal cortex), genetics. Evolution: male aggression over status and mates.

    3 · Drive theories: frustration–aggression hypothesis

    Dollard et al. (1939): frustration always leads to aggression, and aggression always comes from frustration. Berkowitz revised it: frustration causes anger, which leads to aggression especially when aggressive cues are present (the weapons effect, 1967).

    4 · Social learning theory (Bandura)

    Aggression is learned by observing and imitating models, especially rewarded ones.

    Albert Bandura (1961) · Bobo doll studyChildren who watched an adult hit and kick an inflatable Bobo doll copied the same aggressive acts, even the exact words used.Photo: CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons

    General Aggression Model (Anderson & Bushman, 2002)

    Factors that increase aggression

    • Provocation and frustration; hostile attribution bias (seeing others' acts as deliberately hostile)
    • Violent media and video games
    • Heat (more violence on hot days), alcohol, crowding, noise
    • Culture of honour, gender norms, deindividuation in crowds

    Reducing aggression

    • Punishment that is prompt, certain and strong, but it can model aggression too
    • Catharsis is mostly a myth: "venting" (hitting a pillow) usually increases later aggression (Bushman, 2002)
    • Cognitive strategies: apologies, pre-attribution (explaining the situation), delaying responses
    • Teaching social skills, empathy, and non-aggressive models

    Advanced Social Psychology · Unit IV

    Attitudes and Prejudice

    What is an attitude?

    An attitude is an evaluation of any aspect of the social world (a person, object, idea, issue) as positive or negative, favourable or unfavourable.

    G. W. Allport (1935)

    "A mental and neural state of readiness, organised through experience, exerting a directive or dynamic influence upon the individual's response to all objects and situations with which it is related."

    Features
    • Learned, not inborn
    • Have direction (+/−) and strength
    • Fairly stable, but can change
    • Guide behaviour

    ABC model: three components

    RememberAttitudes are as easy as ABC: Affect, Behaviour, Cognition.

    How attitudes form

    Classical conditioning

    Pairing an object with pleasant or unpleasant feelings (adverts with happy music). Can be subliminal.

    Instrumental conditioning

    Being rewarded or punished for expressing an attitude.

    Observational learning

    Copying the attitudes of parents, peers and media figures.

    Social comparison

    Adopting the views of groups we identify with.

    Do attitudes predict behaviour?

    Key study · LaPiere, 1934

    Attitudes ≠ behaviour

    LaPiere travelled across the USA with a Chinese couple. They were refused service only once in 251 places. Yet when he later wrote asking whether these places would serve Chinese guests, over 90% of those that replied said no.

    Attitudes predict behaviour better when they are strong (extreme, certain, from personal experience), specific to the behaviour, and accessible.

    Theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen, 1991)

    Attitude toward the behaviour + subjective norms (what others expect) + perceived behavioural control → intention → behaviour.

    Explicit and implicit attitudes

    Explicit attitudes

    Conscious, deliberate, easy to report. Measured by questionnaires and rating scales (Likert, semantic differential).

    "I believe men and women are equally good at science."

    Implicit attitudes

    Automatic, often unconscious, hard to control. Can differ from explicit attitudes.

    Faster to link "male" with "science" despite saying otherwise.

    Measurement · Greenwald et al., 1998

    Implicit Association Test (IAT)

    Measures how quickly people sort words or faces into paired categories (e.g. "young + good" vs "old + good"). Faster pairing suggests a stronger automatic association.

    Why it matters: explicit attitudes predict deliberate behaviour (voting); implicit attitudes predict spontaneous behaviour (body language, seating distance). The IAT's reliability for predicting an individual's behaviour is debated.

    Attitude change: persuasion

    Yale approach (Hovland, 1950s): who says what to whom

    Communicator (who)

    Credible (expert, trustworthy), attractive and similar sources persuade more. Fast speakers often seem more credible.

    Message (what)

    Messages that don't seem designed to persuade work better. Fear appeals work if they also show how to avoid the danger. Two-sided messages for informed audiences.

    Audience (to whom)

    Mood, intelligence, self-esteem and age affect persuasion. People in a good mood are easier to persuade.

    Sleeper effect: a message from a low-credibility source can become more persuasive over time, as people forget the source.

    Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986)

    A similar model is Chaiken's heuristic–systematic model.

    Other routes to attitude change, and resisting persuasion

    Cognitive dissonance

    Acting against our attitude (with little justification) changes the attitude (see Unit III).

    Resisting persuasion
    • Reactance: pushing back when we feel our freedom is threatened
    • Forewarning: knowing persuasion is coming
    • Selective avoidance of opposing information
    • Inoculation (McGuire): exposure to weak counter-arguments builds resistance, like a vaccine
    • Biased assimilation: evidence against our view seems weaker

    Prejudice

    Prejudice is a negative attitude toward members of a social group, based only on their membership in that group.

    Stereotype · Cognitive

    Beliefs about a group's traits: "group X are lazy".

    Prejudice · Affective

    Negative feelings toward the group.

    Discrimination · Behavioural

    Unfair negative actions toward group members.

    Prejudice follows the ABC structure of attitudes. Examples: caste, religion, gender, region, skin colour, disability, sexual orientation.

    Development: how prejudice is learned

    • Socialisation: children pick up attitudes from parents (by about age 5 they can show racial preferences).
    • Social learning: rewarded for expressing the "right" views; imitating peers.
    • Media: repeated stereotyped portrayals.
    • Social norms: what one's community treats as normal.

    Causes of prejudice

    1 · Realistic conflict theory

    Competition for scarce resources (jobs, land) creates hostility.

    2 · Social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979)

    We boost self-esteem by seeing our group as better than others (in-group favouritism).

    3 · Social categorisation

    Dividing the world into "us" and "them". Out-group homogeneity: "they are all the same".

    4 · Cognitive sources

    Stereotypes save mental effort; illusory correlation links minority groups with rare (negative) behaviour.

    5 · Personality

    Authoritarian personality (Adorno et al., 1950): rigid, obedient to authority, hostile to out-groups. Also social dominance orientation.

    6 · Frustration and scapegoating

    Displacing anger onto a weaker group (e.g. blaming migrants for economic problems).

    Key study · Sherif, 1954

    Robbers Cave experiment

    Boys at a summer camp were split into two groups, "Eagles" and "Rattlers". Competition for prizes quickly produced name-calling and raids. Friendly contact alone didn't fix it; superordinate goals (tasks needing both groups, like fixing the water supply) reduced hostility.

    Henri Tajfel · minimal group paradigm (1970s)Even groups formed on trivial grounds (preferring Klee or Kandinsky paintings) favoured their own members when sharing rewards.Photo: European Association of Social Psychology, CC BY 3.0, Wikimedia Commons

    Effects of prejudice

    Stereotype threat

    Fear of confirming a negative stereotype lowers performance. Black students did worse on a test described as measuring intelligence (Steele & Aronson, 1995).

    Self-fulfilling prophecy

    Low expectations lead to poor treatment, which leads to poor outcomes.

    Health and well-being

    Chronic stress, anxiety, depression, poorer physical health.

    Social

    Discrimination in jobs, housing, education; conflict and violence.

    Reducing prejudice

    Gordon Allport · The Nature of Prejudice (1954)Proposed the contact hypothesis.Photo: C. George Boeree, Free Art License, Wikimedia Commons

    Contact hypothesis: contact works under four conditions

    1 · Equal status

    Groups meet as equals.

    2 · Common goals

    Working toward shared goals.

    3 · Cooperation

    Interdependence, not competition.

    4 · Support of authorities

    Laws, norms, leaders back the contact.

    RememberE-C-C-S: "Equal Classmates Cooperate Successfully."

    Other strategies

    Recategorisation

    Common in-group identity model: turn "us and them" into "we" (e.g. "we are all Indians").

    Jigsaw classroom (Aronson)

    Each student holds one piece of the lesson, so everyone depends on each other.

    Learning not to hate

    Parents and teachers modelling tolerance; education.

    Extended contact

    Knowing that an in-group friend has an out-group friend reduces prejudice.

    Cognitive interventions

    Training to "just say no" to stereotypes; perspective-taking; awareness of implicit bias.

    Social influence

    Showing that peers hold tolerant attitudes changes norms.

    Advanced Social Psychology · Unit V

    Social Influence and Group Processes

    Social influence and conformity

    Social influence is the effort by one or more people to change the attitudes, beliefs or behaviour of others. Conformity means changing our behaviour to match social norms, the rules about how we ought to behave.

    Conformity

    Going along with the group, without being directly asked.

    Compliance

    Agreeing to a direct request.

    Obedience

    Following a command from an authority figure.

    Descriptive norms

    What most people do: "most people here don't litter".

    Injunctive norms

    What people ought to do: "littering is wrong".

    Classic studies

    Key study · Sherif, 1935

    Autokinetic effect

    In a dark room, a still point of light seems to move. Alone, people gave very different estimates of the movement. In groups, their estimates converged on a shared norm, which they kept even when tested alone later. This is informational influence in an ambiguous situation.

    SA
    Solomon Asch (1951, 1956)Line-judgement studies: conformity even when the right answer is obvious.
    Key study · Asch, 1951–56

    Line judgement

    A real participant sat with 6–8 confederates who, on 12 of 18 trials, all gave the same obviously wrong answer about which line matched a standard line. Alone, people made almost no errors (under 1%).

    • Participants conformed on about 37% of critical trials
    • About 75% conformed at least once
    • About 25% never conformed

    Why do we conform? (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955)

    Normative influence

    The desire to be liked and accepted, and fear of rejection. Leads to public compliance without private agreement (Asch).

    Informational influence

    The desire to be right: we treat others as a source of information, especially when uncertain. Leads to real private acceptance (Sherif).

    Factors affecting conformity

    Cohesiveness

    We conform more to groups we like and belong to.

    Group size

    Rises up to about 3–4 people, then levels off.

    Unanimity

    One ally breaks the pressure.

    Public response

    More conformity when answers are public.

    Culture

    Higher in collectivist cultures.

    Task difficulty

    Harder or ambiguous tasks → more informational conformity.

    Conformity bias

    Conformity bias is our tendency to behave like the people around us rather than use our own independent judgement, and to underestimate how much we are influenced. Most people believe "others conform, but I don't".

    Pluralistic ignorance

    Everyone privately disagrees with a norm but wrongly thinks others accept it, so the norm continues (e.g. heavy drinking at college parties).

    Introspection illusion

    We judge ourselves by our inner thoughts ("I chose freely") and others by their behaviour ("they just copied").

    Impact of feedback

    Feedback from the group strongly shapes conformity:

    • Social approval (smiles, praise, inclusion) for agreeing increases conformity. Social disapproval and rejection for disagreeing push people back into line. Even seeing someone else rejected increases conformity.
    • Feedback about accuracy: if people learn the group was often wrong, they conform less; if the group is shown to be right, informational conformity grows.
    • Normative feedback ("your neighbours use less electricity than you") changes behaviour toward the norm; this is used in energy-saving campaigns.
    • Feedback that confirms our own competence (e.g. being told we are an expert) reduces conformity.

    Non-conformity: resisting group pressure

    Need for individuation

    The wish to be distinct and unique from others.

    Need for control

    People with a high desire for control resist pressure more.

    Reactance

    Pushing back when we feel our freedom is threatened.

    Social support

    Having even one ally who disagrees with the group.

    Idiosyncrasy credits

    People who have conformed in the past "earn credits" that let them deviate later without rejection.

    Power

    High-power people conform less.

    Minority influence · Moscovici, 1969

    The consistent minority

    A minority that consistently called blue slides "green" influenced the majority's later colour judgements. A consistent, confident minority can change majority views, often slowly and privately.

    Obedience to authority

    Obedience is following the direct orders of an authority figure. Can ordinary people be made to harm others simply because they are told to?

    Key study · Milgram, 1963

    The "shock" experiment

    Participants, as "teachers", were told to give electric shocks to a "learner" (a confederate) for each wrong answer, rising in 15-volt steps from 15 V to 450 V. No real shocks were given, but the learner screamed, protested, then fell silent. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter used prods such as "The experiment requires that you continue."

    Psychiatrists predicted almost no one would go to the end. In fact 65% obeyed all the way to 450 V.

    Why do people obey?

    Agentic state

    People see themselves as an "agent" carrying out another's wishes, so they shift responsibility to the authority.

    Legitimate authority

    Uniforms, titles, a prestigious setting (Yale) increase obedience.

    Gradual escalation (foot-in-the-door)

    Small 15-volt steps made each next step feel only slightly worse.

    Norms of obedience

    We are taught from childhood to obey parents, teachers, police.

    Resisting destructive obedience

    • Remind people that they are responsible for harm
    • Teach that blind obedience is inappropriate; question authority's motives
    • Disobedient models: seeing others refuse
    • Know about the research: awareness itself helps

    Ethics and replication: Milgram's study caused severe stress and used deception, and led to stricter ethics rules. Burger (2009) ran a safer partial replication (stopping at 150 V) and found obedience rates similar to Milgram's.

    What is a group?

    A group is two or more people who interact, are interdependent, share common goals, and see themselves as a unit. A crowd at a bus stop is not a group; a cricket team is.

    Entitativity

    How much a collection of people is seen as a real, unified group. High for families and teams; low for people in a queue.

    Roles

    Expected behaviours for each position (captain, secretary).

    Status

    Rank or prestige within the group.

    Norms

    Rules about how members should behave.

    Cohesiveness

    Forces that bind members together; liking and commitment.

    Why do people join groups? (functions)

    Belonging

    Basic need for affiliation, love and acceptance.

    Self-knowledge

    Groups help us know who we are (social comparison).

    Identity and self-esteem

    Group membership adds to self-concept and pride.

    Achieving goals

    Doing together what we can't do alone.

    Security and protection

    Safety in numbers, support in hard times.

    Information

    Learning from others, reducing uncertainty.

    Social loafing

    Social loafing is the tendency to put in less effort when working in a group on a shared task, where individual contributions are pooled and not identifiable, than when working alone.

    Key study · Ringelmann, 1880s (published 1913)

    Rope pulling

    As more men pulled a rope together, the force per person fell.

    Key study · Latané, Williams & Harkins, 1979

    Clapping and shouting

    Blindfolded students wearing headphones shouted or clapped "alone" or "with others". They made less noise per person when they believed others were also shouting, even when they were actually alone. So the drop is due to reduced effort, not just poor coordination.

    Why it happens: collective effort model (Karau & Williams, 1993)

    People work hard only when they believe their effort will matter for an outcome they value. In groups, each person's effort feels less important and less noticed.

    Reducing social loafing

    Identifiability

    Make each person's contribution visible.

    Commitment

    Increase commitment to the task's success.

    Importance

    Make the task meaningful and valued.

    Unique contribution

    Give each member a distinct, necessary role.

    Small groups and cohesion

    Friends and small teams loaf less.

    Loafing is weaker in collectivist cultures and among women. The opposite, social compensation, happens when people work harder to make up for weak teammates on an important task.

    Group decision-making

    How groups reach decisions: social decision schemes

    Majority-wins

    The view held by most members is chosen. The most common rule.

    Truth-wins

    The correct answer wins once its correctness is recognised.

    First-shift

    The group adopts the direction of the first person who changes position.

    Group polarisation

    Group discussion makes members' views more extreme in the direction they already leaned. First found as the "risky shift" (Stoner, 1961), then shown to work in either direction (Moscovici & Zavalloni, 1969).

    Why? (1) Social comparison: members want to seem "better" than average in the valued direction. (2) Persuasive arguments: discussion brings out more arguments on the favoured side.

    Groupthink (Irving Janis, 1972)

    IJ
    Irving JanisStudied disastrous US decisions such as the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion.

    Groupthink happens when a highly cohesive group cares more about agreement than about making the right decision.

    Causes
    • High cohesiveness
    • Isolation from outside views
    • A strong, directive leader
    • High stress, time pressure
    Symptoms
    • Illusion of invulnerability
    • Belief in the group's morality
    • Collective rationalisation
    • Stereotyping opponents
    • Pressure on dissenters
    • Self-censorship
    • Illusion of unanimity
    • Self-appointed "mindguards"

    Preventing groupthink

    • The leader stays impartial and invites criticism
    • Assign a devil's advocate; encourage authentic dissent
    • Use sub-groups and outside experts
    • Hold a "second-chance" meeting before finalising

    Other problems in group decisions

    Biased information sampling

    Groups discuss information everyone already shares and ignore unique information held by individuals (the "hidden profile", Stasser & Titus, 1985).

    Escalation of commitment

    Continuing to invest in a failing decision because of what has already been spent (sunk cost).
